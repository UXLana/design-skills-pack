# Case-study evidence interview

Read this when the case is missing evidence about the problem, decisions, influence or outcome.

## How to conduct it

Read the existing record first. Identify the most consequential missing evidence, ask one short question, and wait. Follow the answer before moving to the next topic. The prompts below are an internal menu, not a questionnaire to paste into chat.

Ask for specifics without implying what the answer should be. Restate an important causal claim briefly so the author can correct it. Do not reinterpret uncertainty as success. Do not require a dramatic disagreement, numerical metric or failure if none occurred.

If the author wants a draft now, draft with clear gaps in the private handoff. Do not silently fill them with plausible product language.

## 1. Find the actual problem

Start: “Before this work, what was one thing a person was trying to do that was unnecessarily difficult?”

Possible follow-ups, one at a time:

- Who was that person, and what situation put them in that difficulty?
- Walk me through a specific example of how they handled it then.
- How did you learn about it?
- What happened when it went wrong?
- How often did it occur, if known?

Separate customer-observed pain, stakeholder-reported pain and a team's opportunity hypothesis. Product requirements alone do not validate the pain. The business problem and the user's problem may differ; capture both where relevant.

## 2. Establish the stakes and constraints

Start: “Why did this become important to solve at that point?”

Follow-ups: what was at risk, what deadline or dependency mattered, which constraints shaped the approach, and what was outside the author's control? Record unknown commercial details as unknown.

## 3. Reconstruct a consequential decision

Start: “What was one decision that most changed the direction of the work?”

Follow-ups: what options were being considered, what evidence mattered, who made the final call, what the author recommended, what was given up, and why that tradeoff was acceptable then?

Capture one decision fully before collecting more. A list of screens or activities is not yet an explanation of judgment. If no alternatives are documented or remembered, say so; do not invent a rejected direction.

## 4. Reveal personal contribution and influence

Start: “What did you personally do that changed that decision or helped it move forward?”

If the answer is “I aligned stakeholders,” ask which decision needed alignment. Then follow up on the perspectives involved, what the author brought to the discussion and what changed afterward.

If the answer is “I led the work,” ask what responsibility the author held in a specific moment. Distinguish setting direction, conducting research, designing, implementing, facilitating and making the final decision. Preserve collaborators' contributions. Do not assume influence required conflict; enabling a decision or making an ambiguity understandable can matter.

## 5. Connect the decision to the artifact

Start: “Where can someone see the effect of that decision in the product?”

Follow-ups: which interaction demonstrates it, what changed from the prior behavior, what artifact survives and whether that artifact is from the relevant time. Name the difference between an original version and a later replay adaptation.

Use a screenshot, prototype state or diagram because it helps the reader understand the decision. Give it a caption explaining that relationship. Do not let available screenshots choose the story by default.

## 6. Establish the result and the limits

Start: “What changed afterward, and how do you know?”

Follow-ups: what customers or colleagues could do differently, which behavior was observed, which result was measured, when it was measured and what evidence survives. Ask what other factors contributed before attributing a team outcome to an individual action.

A business milestone, adoption by colleagues, implementation decision or observed usability improvement may be useful qualitative evidence. Distinguish these from numerical performance metrics. If the work remained a prototype, describe the decision it enabled and its limitations without inventing production success.

Ask about a lesson or a later correction only when it adds substance. The author may decline to discuss sensitive context; do not transfer it into publication copy.

## Build a decision card from the answers

For each well-supported decision, summarize:

1. The specific problem or tension.
2. Options and constraints that actually existed.
3. Evidence available at the time.
4. The author's action, influence and ownership, alongside collaborators.
5. The choice and accepted tradeoff.
6. The artifact showing the resulting behavior.
7. The observed consequence and its evidence limits.

Not every case needs every field on the page. Missing answers remain open in the private record; they are not a reason to fabricate a complete card.

## Make decisions easy to follow

Make the reasoning easy to follow. Explain the problem, options, the author's influence, choice, tradeoff and resulting change in a connected account. Use plain language so readers can understand why a decision was made and what it affected.

Preserve the existing approved design. This guidance improves writing and does not prescribe art direction, layout or where text, images, diagrams or prototypes appear.

Select the consequential decisions instead of recounting every activity. Explain technical details only when they clarify a constraint or tradeoff. Keep collaborators and uncertainty visible. If an intermediate link in the reasoning is missing, ask about it or mark the gap; do not invent a smoother story.

## Completion rule

Draft when there is enough evidence to explain a concrete problem, the author's contribution and at least one consequential decision with a supported result or an explicit limit. If those remain unclear and the author ends the interview, deliver a provisional outline with the few questions that matter most. Do not certify a story as publication-ready merely because the layout is finished.
